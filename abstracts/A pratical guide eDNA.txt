A Practical Guide to DNA-Based Methods for Biodiversity Assessment

This document summarises the scientific consensus relating to every step of the field and laboratory workflows involved in the most common types of samples and analyses for DNA-based biodiversity assessment. DNA-based methods for species detection and identification have revolutionised the ability to assess biodiversity in terrestrial, freshwater and marine ecosystems. Starting from the seminal study that used eDNA to detect invasive american bullfrogs in France, research over the last decade has demonstrated the power of these approaches for surveying a wide range of species and groups. Early applications included the use of eDNA to monitor Asian Carp in the USA, followed by tests designed for threatened and invasive species including New Zealand mudsnails, american crayfish, gammarids, and great crested newts.

As the field developed rapidly and approaches were applied to various research and monitoring objectives, significant methodological variation was introduced at all stages of the workflow. While consensus on scientific best-practice now exists in many areas, environmental practitioners and policy makers are increasingly integrating DNA-based methods into routine monitoring applications including protected species licensing, statutory monitoring, and environmental impact assessment. This requires that scientific robustness be balanced with consideration of practical realities faced by environmental managers, with increased emphasis on robustness, replicability, traceability and ease-of-use.

This publication is an output from EU COST Action DNAqua-Net, working towards incorporating molecular monitoring tools for Biological Quality Elements into the Water Framework Directive and Marine Strategy Framework Directive in Europe.
